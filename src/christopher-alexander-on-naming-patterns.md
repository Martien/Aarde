# Christopher Alexander on naming patterns
Christopher Alexander, in his work with collaborators on *A Pattern Language* (1977) and the companion volume *The Timeless Way of Building*, treats the **naming** of patterns as a central and practical element of building a usable "language" for design.

Each pattern in the language follows a consistent format that begins with:

- A short, familiar, descriptive **name** (or phrase).
- Often accompanied by a rating (asterisks indicating confidence in its universality).
- A sensitizing picture or illustration.
- Context, problem statement, discussion with examples, solution, sketch, and cross-references to other patterns.

## Why Names Matter
Alexander emphasizes that **pattern names must be**:
- **easy to remember** and
- **highly descriptive**.

This serves several key purposes:

- They function like words in a spoken language, allowing people (individuals, teams, or communities) to refer to recurring design problems and solutions conversationally, without needing to restate the full problem-solution pair each time.
- When designing collaboratively or iteratively, the names create a shared vocabulary. You can say something like “apply {window place} here” or “ensure {a place to wait},” and others immediately grasp the core idea.
- The name is usually more indicative of the **solution** than the problem, helping it evoke the desired configuration or feeling directly (e.g., {window place} suggests a specific way to position windows for comfort and light; {a place to wait} captures the humane design of waiting areas in various settings like bus stops or hospitals).

Examples from Alexander's patterns illustrate this:
- **{window place}** — Defines comfortable seating near windows.
- **{a place to wait}** — Addresses the recurring need for humane waiting spaces.
- **{high places}** — Captures the human instinct for elevated vantage points.

These names are concise yet evocative, general enough to apply across scales or contexts while specific enough to guide action.

## Additional Guidance on Naming
Alexander notes that users of the language should feel free to adapt or create their own patterns, and if they do so, they should **change the name** accordingly to clearly capture the modified or new idea. This keeps the language alive and personal rather than rigid.

In secondary literature and explanations of Alexandrian patterns, this principle is often summarized as requiring names that are:
- **Memorable** (short and familiar, like everyday language).
- **Descriptive** (they "tell the story" of the solution in a nutshell).
- **Useful for reference** within the network of patterns, where the language forms an interconnected web rather than a linear list.

The name is not merely a label; it helps embed the pattern into everyday thinking and design discourse, supporting the broader goal of empowering people to create buildings and places with the “quality without a name” – a living, whole, comfortable feeling that defies easy definition but is recognizable when present.

In short, Alexander views effective naming as essential for turning isolated insights about recurring problems/solutions into **a genuine, shareable *language* that people can use fluidly to generate coherent, human-centered designs**. This aspect heavily influenced later fields like software design patterns, where the same emphasis on clear, evocative names persists.

Christopher Alexander (with his co-authors) explains aspects of **why pattern names matter**—particularly their role in making the language usable as a shared vocabulary, their need to be **easy to remember** and **highly descriptive**, and the recommendation to change a name when adapting a pattern—in **A Pattern Language: Towns, Buildings, Construction** (1977).

This discussion appears in the introductory material, specifically in the section titled something like “**Choosing a Language for Your Subject**” or the general instructions on using/adapting the pattern language (around pages xxxviii–xl in many editions, just before or within the “Summary of the Language” and before the patterns themselves begin).

Key excerpt (paraphrased and directly supported by the text):

> “This makes it necessary for pattern names to be easy to remember and highly descriptive."  

And,

>“And, it will be most concrete of all, if you change the name of the pattern too — so that it captures your own changes clearly.”

The names function like words in a natural language, enabling people to refer to complex design ideas conversationally (e.g., {window place} or {a place to wait}) without restating the full problem and solution each time. This supports collaborative design and turns the collection into a genuine *language* rather than a mere list.

**The Timeless Way of Building** (1979), the companion theoretical volume, provides the broader philosophical context for pattern languages (including how they generate coherent designs and the {quality without a name}), but the specific practical guidance on **naming**—why the names must be memorable/descriptive and how to adapt them—is in **A Pattern Language** itself.

The pattern format in the book reinforces this: each of the 253 patterns starts with its short, evocative **name** in smallcaps, followed by a picture, context, problem, discussion, solution, etc. The name is positioned as the entry point that makes the entire idea quickly accessible and referenceable.
